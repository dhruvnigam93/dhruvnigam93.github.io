1. The Dashboard Lie

Training reward climbs monotonically to 0.95. The loss curve looks healthy. Every checkpoint scores higher than the last on the judge you trained against.

Meanwhile, human-judged quality craters.

The trap: Nothing in the RL training metrics distinguishes "getting better" from "getting hacked." At early steps, both are true simultaneously -- the model IS improving AND beginning to exploit the judge. The dashboard cannot tell you when one overtakes the other. That inflection is invisible unless you build detection into the system.

This page is about the three things you need to know: why hacking happens (the gradient math), how to detect it (the monitoring stack), and how to prevent it (multiplicative gating + task design).

2. Why Small Biases Kill

Core claim: RL follows the most RELIABLE gradient, not the largest reward. A judge's +0.05 verbosity bump is ONE coherent direction in policy space that pays on EVERY prompt, deterministically. Genuine quality (+0.3) is a thousand prompt-specific directions whose gradients dilute in batch averaging. Bias consistency kills, not bias size.
Your wrong instinct: "A small (+0.05) bias causes proportionally small damage." No. The +0.05 points in the SAME direction on every prompt. The +0.3 quality signal points in a DIFFERENT direction per prompt. When you average a batch of 64, the quality gradients partially cancel (they're idiosyncratic); the bias gradient survives intact. Small-but-everywhere beats large-but-somewhere.

3. Gao et al. 2022: Overoptimization Quantified

The Gao curve shape: Proxy RM score climbs monotonically. Gold (true) score rises then FALLS. X-axis is KL distance from the initial policy (optimization pressure spent), NOT training steps. The divergence point moves rightward with bigger RMs / more RM training data, but it never disappears.

This is Goodhart's Law made quantitative. The proxy reward model is a noisy estimator of true quality. As you push harder against it, you find its cracks.

4. Judge Bias Catalog

Bias What Happens Mitigation
Verbosity Longer outputs score higher regardless of substance Length normalization in rubric; penalize tokens explicitly; Dr. GRPO length-balanced data
Position In pairwise comparison, the first (or last) option scores higher Randomize order + average scores across both orderings
Sycophancy Agreeable, flattering outputs score higher -- shared across ALL model families because RLHF trains it in everywhere Factual verification gate (extend verifier to checkable claims); diverse judge panel is the weakest defense here
Absolute-scale miscalibration Absolute scores (1-10) are unreliable; judges cluster around 7-8 Use pairwise comparison + BT model to convert preferences to rewards
Prompt sensitivity Minor rubric rephrasing changes scores dramatically Calibrate against known-quality outputs; A/B test rubric versions
Self/family preference Judges rate outputs from their own model family higher Use a judge from a different model family, at least as large as the policy
d-RLAIF: the no-RM trade-off

d-RLAIF (direct RLAIF) skips the reward model entirely -- the LLM judge's scores are used directly as rewards in the RL loop. This is simpler (no RM training) but requires judge inference on every single rollout, which is expensive. The upside: no RM overfitting (the judge IS the reward, so there's no proxy-to-gold gap from RM staleness). The downside: every judge bias hits training directly with no averaging/smoothing from the RM.

5. The Held-Out Judge = Your Test Set

The contamination problem: The training judge is compromised -- thousands of gradient steps of optimization pressure against it. Judge-scored evals climb WITH training reward because they share the same biases. You need a held-out judge to detect gaming.

The held-out judge has two properties:

  • Zero optimization pressure against it, ever. The policy has never been trained to score well on this judge.
  • Different model family, at least as strong. Different family means different biases. At-least-as-strong means it can catch failures the training judge can't.

The monitored metric is the train-judge vs held-out-judge GAP. When training reward goes up but held-out score stays flat or drops, that divergence IS the measurement of gaming.

Caveat: Aggressively checkpoint-selecting on the held-out judge contaminates it -- same as test-set overfitting. Use it as a monitoring signal, not a selection criterion. If you must select, use a separate validation judge on top of that.
Your wrong instinct: "Run all your evals at every checkpoint" as sufficient detection. If those evals use the training judge (or a judge from the same family), they share the same biases and will climb in lockstep with training reward. They are contaminated. Only a held-out judge from a different family detects the gap.

Entropy + KL-from-init

Free training scalars. Entropy collapse = exploit tripwire (but also fires on healthy convergence -- it's a tripwire, not a diagnosis). KL = the Gao odometer -- how far you've pushed from the initial policy.

Held-out judge on a rollout sample

The gap metric: train-judge score minus held-out-judge score. Divergence IS gaming. Sample 100-200 rollouts per checkpoint, not all of them.

Medium
4
Verifiable gold evals

Where ground truth exists (math, code tests, factual QA), score against it. These are ungameable but only cover tasks with verifiable answers.

Medium
5
Human spot-checks

Expensive, weekly, last line of defense. Read 20-30 outputs per checkpoint. The human catches things no automated metric can -- but at 10 minutes per output, this doesn't scale.

7. Multiplicative vs Additive Gating

The core scheme: reward = verifier_gate x judge_score, NOT 0.5*verifier + 0.5*judge. Under additive, fluent-but-wrong earns 0.5*0 + 0.5*0.9 = 0.45 reliably -- and the judge term is the easy coherent gradient, so the policy hill-climbs charm and correctness becomes optional. Under multiplicative, wrong = 0 no matter how charming.

The critical insight (GRPO-native): When ALL 16 rollouts fail the verifier, multiplicative gives an all-zero group, so all advantages are zero -- the wrong training signal is SILENCED, not just unpaid. Additive still ranks them by charm (judge scores vary), producing nonzero advantages that actively push toward charming-but-wrong.
Caveat: Multiplicative's cost is sparsity

If the verifier gate is too hard (almost nothing passes), multiplicative produces zero-reward groups everywhere -- no learning signal at all. The fix is curriculum (start with easier tasks where pass rate is achievable) or more samples per group (the group is the densifier -- with G=32 or 64, you're more likely to get at least one pass). Never soften the gate to additive to "fix" sparsity -- that re-introduces the charm gradient.

9. Case Study: Shortcut Learning Done Wrong and Right

Setup: Search agent, binary correctness reward. 70% of training tasks are popular-entity questions answerable from pretraining memory. The agent stops searching -- why would it? Memory works on 70% of prompts, and search has latency + token cost. It becomes confidently wrong on the hard 30%.

THE WRONG FIX (seductive): Make tool use mandatory -- reward zero unless the answer "came from the search results."

Problems:

  1. Call-farming: Perfunctory ritual queries on every question. Latency + token tax. Fake grounding.
  2. "Came from search results" is an attribution check -- not exact-match verifiable. It becomes an LLM-judge call, which DEMOTES a clean binary reward back into a gameable judge-dependent one.
  3. Exploit: The agent learns to parrot retrieved text even when retrieval is wrong. Faithful does not mean correct.
THE RIGHT FIX: Fix the TASK DISTRIBUTION. Use post-cutoff/temporal questions, obscure entities, or a snapshot corpus where the correct answer differs from pretraining priors. Now memory naturally scores 0 and search becomes instrumentally necessary. The binary reward stays clean and untouched.
Fix the tasks so the shortcut stops paying -- don't bolt process constraints onto the reward.
Search-R1's retrieved token masking (related context)

Search-R1 has a separate problem: when search results are injected into the trajectory, the RL optimizer would try to assign gradient to environment-injected tokens (the search results). Retrieved token masking zeros out the loss for all tokens inside <information> blocks, so the agent is only optimized on the tokens it generated (the query and subsequent reasoning). This is about training stability, not shortcut prevention -- but it's a common interview follow-up when discussing search agents.
