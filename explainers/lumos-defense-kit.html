0 · The 90-second opener (lab register)

Lead with the mechanism, not the scale. A lab hears "foundation model" at this size and rolls its eyes; it hears the cross-attention structure and leans in.

LUMOS is an encoder-decoder transformer for multi-task user-behavior prediction at Dream11 — it replaced hand-engineered, task-specific models across the platform. The part I'd call novel: the decoder cross-attends from known future event context to encoded user history — queries come from the future sporting calendar, keys and values from the user's past — with decoder self-attention removed, so each future day's prediction depends only on history and its own context. That lets the model condition on scheduled exogenous events, which a decoder-only stack structurally cannot do. Tokenization is one continuous vector per user-day; the loss is uncertainty-weighted multi-task (Kendall et al.). Trained on 250M users × 360-day sequences — about 50 TB — on 8×A100 in 72 hours. Against the specialist baselines it gained +0.025 ROC-AUC, and in a randomized A/B on the promotion system — ~700K users per arm, 18 days — it drove +3.15% DAU with −2.47% offer spend.

Retire the phrase "foundation model." Say "domain-specific multi-task sequence model." Understating scale while overstating precision is the researcher's tell — in the good sense.

2 · Architecture — the two-minute walkthrough

Dream11 had dozens of small task-specific models — same features, ad-hoc engineering, different targets. Redundant, and operationally expensive. LUMOS collapses them into one sequence model. The encoder ingests the user's history: one token per day, each a continuous vector of that day's aggregated activity plus the event context for that day. The decoder's sequence is the future event calendar — for each upcoming day, an event vector summarizing what's scheduled. Queries come from those future-day vectors; cross-attention reads the encoded history; there's no decoder self-attention, so each future day is conditionally independent given history and its own context. The decoder output feeds task-specific heads — kept deliberately deep so the shared embedding stays generic. Those embeddings became a product of their own: teams train downstream models on them.
"Why cross-attention at all? Decoder-only would do next-action prediction fine."

Because the future is not exchangeable with the past here. User activity at Dream11 is driven by the sporting calendar — a known, scheduled, exogenous signal. A decoder-only stack can only condition on what has already happened; the cross-attention structure lets every prediction condition on what is about to happen ("IPL final on Saturday") without leaking it into the history encoding. That's the architectural contribution — and the ablation backs it up (§11: removing future event context hurts more than removing both).

3 · The token — and the sparsity concession

One token = one user-day: a fixed-length continuous vector of daily aggregates — contests joined, deposits, app opens — plus that day's event context. There's no discrete vocabulary and no embedding lookup; it's a linear projection into the model dimension. I'd call it aggregation, not feature engineering: no domain knowledge, no task-specific choices — the same recipe would work unchanged for e-commerce or food delivery. The real feature-engineering cost was always deciding which features encode domain knowledge; here there are none.
"85–90% of your positions are empty days. You're burning ~99% of attention FLOPs on padding."

Own it — this was handled honestly in the grill and it landed:

Average activation is 10–15%, so yes — most positions are empty days, and most attention pairs are empty-empty. Two reasons we kept them. First, an empty day carries signal: the token still contains that day's event context, so "didn't show up on a big match day" is information the model sees explicitly. Second, uniform daily spacing kept the backbone identical to a standard LLM stack — no custom position embeddings for irregular gaps. I'll concede the cost plainly: we never ran the compressed-vs-padded ablation, because event-time compression needs a different positional scheme — it's an architecture change, not a flag. It's the first thing I'd explore in a v2.

Don't wave at "95% of use cases are daily-level" as if it settles the intra-day ordering question — deposit-then-play and play-then-deposit differ on a gambling platform. The honest line: daily aggregation was a tractability choice, validated only indirectly by downstream wins.

5 · Scaling laws — say the ratio, never the percentages

This broke in both sessions: per-doubling percentages got computed wrong out loud, twice. The fix is structural — the ratio of exponents is the insight, so lead with it and never quote per-doubling arithmetic from memory.

We fit L = E + a·N−0.15 + b·D−0.05 — with an irreducible term. The point wasn't the absolute optimum, it was the ratio: the parameter exponent came out ~3× the data exponent with comparable coefficients, so at our operating point we were far more parameter-starved than data-starved. That told us to scale the model, not collect more history. We stopped at the shipped size because the lift was already large enough to deploy and it fit serving with zero pipeline change — we knew we were leaving loss on the table, and scaling N is the current workstream.

Volunteer the limitation before they find it — this is the strongest scientist-not-salesperson signal available in the whole story:

The scaling law is the weakest result in the paper, and I'll flag it myself: it's fit on the uncertainty-weighted composite loss, and because the sigmas are learned, runs aren't strictly comparable — a model can lower that number by inflating its uncertainties. R² on the model-size fit was 0.62 over a 3× range. If I reran it, I'd fit on a fixed held-out per-task metric — mean BCE across the classification tasks.

6 · Multi-task loss — the best answer of the grill, kept sharp

The heads span classification and regression on wildly different scales, so a naive sum lets one task's gradient own the trunk. We used uncertainty weighting — Kendall, Gal & Cipolla — where per-task sigmas are learned on the fly and hard tasks aren't over-penalized. And we verified the trunk wasn't being sacrificed the direct way: single-head specialist models trained at smaller scale as controls, confirming the uncertainty-weighted trunk gave up almost nothing per-task. The heads branch deep off the decoder embedding, which keeps that embedding generic — generic enough that we later shipped it as a standalone product for downstream teams.

Know the follow-up: how many heads, which loss per head (BCE for the binary tasks, regression losses for the continuous ones — have the exact count from the paper's 13 dimensions ready). Not having the head count was the only dent in an otherwise winning answer.

7 · "Replaced 50+ models" — what replaced means, and the missing number

"Replaced" means the legacy models' consumers were cut over to LUMOS heads or LUMOS embeddings — not that 50 models vanished on day one. Where we had baselines: +0.025 average ROC-AUC on the binary tasks, −4.6% MAPE on the continuous ones. Not everything improved — a handful of regression tasks got worse — and we shipped anyway, because one model in production versus fifty, each with its own feature pipeline and on-call surface, is an enormous operational win. For tasks where the specialist still won, the path was: train on LUMOS embeddings rather than keep the bespoke pipeline.
The worst-regression number is still missing, and every serious interviewer asks for it. "Some regressed" without a name and a magnitude reads as either not knowing your own system or hiding something. 45 minutes with the per-task eval table: pull the two or three worst tasks, their deltas, and what was decided for each. The maintenance-consolidation argument only works after you've shown you know exactly what it cost.

8 · The A/B — narrate the scope in your FIRST sentence

The design is genuinely good. The risk is entirely in scoping: the resume reads as a platform-wide DAU lift; the truth is a promotion-surface experiment. Interviewers forgive scope; they don't forgive discovering it themselves.

Let me scope the +3.15% precisely, because it's a surface-level result, not a platform number. Dream11's promotion system decides discounts from churn risk and pay/deposit propensity. We swapped exactly those input predictions — legacy task-specific models out, LUMOS heads in — and randomized users: ~700K per arm, 18 days, during IPL. Treatment DAU came out +3.15% over control with offer spend −2.47%. IPL is actually the low-variance window — a match every day, DAU extremely stable, so effects this size are unambiguous; p < 0.01. What I can't claim: a measured platform-wide lift after full rollout — once you deploy to everyone there's no counterfactual left. The A/B is the evidence; the rollout was the bet on it.
Memorize the actual confidence interval, not the variance argument. "I don't remember, but it's highly significant" was said in a grill. Pull CI and p-value from the experiment dashboard and have both cold.

9 · Spend down AND DAU up — the mechanism, worded carefully

These normally trade off, so the mechanism question is guaranteed. The answer is good — it just got garbled live (a "30% → 45%" example that inverted mid-sentence). Fixed version, slow on the numbers:

You can always buy DAU with more offer spend — the policy sits on that trade-off curve. What moved both in the right direction was churn accuracy improving exactly where the money leaks: the boundary band. Users the old model put around 30–45% churn risk are the decision boundary — that score decides whether they get a promotion. Get it wrong on someone who was coming back anyway, and they take the discount without it changing anything: pure spend, zero incremental DAU. LUMOS, seeing the full raw behavior sequence instead of hand-picked features, got precisely those ambiguous cases right more often. So promotions stopped going to users who didn't need them — spend fell — while true at-risk users kept getting them — DAU rose.

10 · Accio — reframed around what it actually bought

The old bullet implied a throughput win; under questioning, MFU was 10–15% before and after. That contradiction loses the room. The reframe — which is also just the truth — makes it a build-vs-buy judgment call with a profiled bottleneck:

Everything at Dream11 lives in Delta tables, and the existing streaming loaders — Petastorm, Mosaic's — all forced an intermediate converted copy with tighter schema constraints than Delta itself, and gave almost no hook for load-time transforms. So I built Accio: a Delta-native streaming loader that shards directly into the GPUs. Throughput came out comparable to Petastorm, marginally slower than Mosaic — and I took that trade deliberately: no second copy of a 50TB dataset, no schema compromise, and date-indexed enrichment at load time that I couldn't express in either alternative. And throughput genuinely wasn't the critical path: MFU was ~10–15%, bounded by the single-process coupling of loading and training, not by format. Fixing that needs parallel loader workers feeding each GPU process — which is exactly why we were evaluating Ray Data next.

Naming the real bottleneck is what turns this from defensive to profiled. "I built" is correct here — keep claiming it; it was the one clean ownership claim in the session.

Have a bug story. "Describe one bug you personally debugged in Accio" got "I can't remember specifically" — fatal for an I-built-this claim. You clearly fought shard-boundary / epoch-repeat / worker-duplication issues (you said so unprompted). Reconstruct ONE: symptom → how you found it → the fix. 60 seconds, rehearsed.

11 · The lab-facing gems — strongest results, currently unsold

Neither of these is on the resume, and they're what a researcher actually wants to discuss:

Appendix B.2 — the embeddings beat the specialists at their own game. Multi-task LUMOS embeddings outperform single-task specialist models on the specialists' own tasks (+7.8%, +7.4%). That's the transfer-learning claim most multi-task papers wish they had.

Appendix B.4 — the event-context ablation is counterintuitive. Removing past event context or future event context each hurts more than removing both. The two context streams are complements — the model uses them jointly, not additively. A real, mechanistic, slightly weird finding — exactly the thing to volunteer to a lab.

12 · Questions that broke — each one rebuilt

"Where do 1.7 trillion tokens come from?"

Ended a grill with "I'll get back to you" — on a resume number. Rebuilt:

Token means one user-day, and there are ~90B of those — 250M users by a 360-day window. The 1.7T is the scalar count: each day-token is a ~20-dimensional feature vector, and 90B × ~19–22 dims lands at 1.7–2T values, about 50TB. On the resume I say 90B tokens; I keep 1.7T only as "scalar features," never as tokens.
"What did your fit say the compute-optimal size was?"

Don't produce per-doubling percentages (§5). The shape: state the fitted form with the intercept, give the exponent ratio and its decision ("scale N, not D"), own that the shipped size was a deployment choice below the fit's optimum, and name the caveat (composite loss, learned sigmas, R² 0.62) unprompted.

"Which task regressed the most, by how much — and did you ship it regressed?"

Open item (§7). Until the eval table is pulled, the honest bridge:

I don't have the per-task deltas memorized and I won't guess them. What I can tell you is the decision rule: a task owner could keep their specialist if it beat the LUMOS head on their holdout — and the migration path we pushed was retraining their model on LUMOS embeddings, which in our ablations beat specialists even on their own tasks.

"I don't have that number, here's what I do have" costs far less than answering a different question — which is what happened twice.

"What was the CI on +3.15%?"

Open item (§8). Have the interval and p-value cold; the IPL-stability variance argument is supporting material, not a substitute.

"GPU utilization before and after Accio?" / "One bug you debugged in it?"

Utilization: answer is §10 — same MFU before and after, and that's fine because the loader was never the bottleneck and you profiled it. Bug story: open item, reconstruct one.

13 · Delivery rules (from both debriefs)

1 · Structure to drill: claim → number → mechanism → caveat. Four sentences, then stop and let them ask. Every grill answer arrived at its point 45–60 seconds in; interviewers judge in the first 15.

2 · Signs flip under pressure. "AUC reduction" for improvement; MAE direction inverted; 30→45% garbled. Slow down physically on any sentence with a metric in it.

3 · Answer the question that was asked. Twice a fact question (worst regression, Accio bug) got a rationale answer. If the fact is missing, say so in one clean sentence, then give what you do have.

4 · Don't agree with false critiques of your own work. A grill "corrected" the scaling fit for missing an intercept — it had one — and the concession came instantly. In a real interview that's how a defensible result becomes an indefensible one. If you know your work, defend it.

5 · Kill the "right?" tic — roughly once per 30 seconds in transcripts. It reads as seeking agreement.

6 · "I" for what you did. "I built Accio" landed. The scaling law, the A/B, the multi-task design were all "we" — claim your part in each, one sentence per.
