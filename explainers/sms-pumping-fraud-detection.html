1. Frame the problem before designing (first 5 min)

What's actually happening: the attacker and a carrier (or aggregator) split the SMS termination fee. Bots hit a customer's signup/login form, request OTPs to numbers the attacker controls in that carrier's ranges. Nobody ever completes verification. The customer pays Twilio, Twilio pays the route, the colluding carrier kicks back to the attacker. It's revenue-share fraud, not account-takeover — that shapes everything: the adversary's objective is message VOLUME to specific destinations, not access.

The fraud loop, animated

Pin the constraints out loud (ask, don't assume):

  • Scale: ~5B verifications/yr → ~160/s avg, ~2K RPS peak. 300K+ tenants with baselines differing by orders of magnitude; new tenants daily.
  • Latency: decision is inline in the send path, <100ms p99 budget.
  • Labels: no ground truth. Conversion (OTP completed) arrives ≤10 min, pumped traffic converts ≈0%. Disputes arrive days later, sparse.
  • Costs: FN = $0.01–0.50/SMS × millions (Twitter: ~$60M/yr) + strategic churn risk of the customer leaving Twilio. FP = a legit user can't log in → customer's LTV/churn + trust in Twilio. Asymmetric AND customer-dependent.
  • Prevalence: ~5% of A2P globally, but bursty — during an attack, majority of an app's traffic to a range is fraud. Class balance is regime-dependent, not stationary.

Objective statement: maximize fraud-$ blocked subject to a per-customer FP budget, under 100ms, with per-tenant tunability. Not "classify fraud" — decide, per request, whether the expected value of sending is positive.

2. Decision economics FIRST (this is what makes it staff-level)

Block iff p(fraud) × cost_FN > (1 − p(fraud)) × cost_FP.

  • cost_FN ≈ destination price (known upfront, $0.01–0.50) + amortized strategic cost.
  • cost_FP ≈ customer's value of a completed verification — varies 100x by customer (bank login vs game signup). Twilio can't know it → so expose it as a knob. That IS the product insight: Basic/Standard/Max protection modes are presets of cost_FP, which map to score thresholds. I propose tiered modes; the interviewer's hidden card (real product: Basic <0.1% FP, Standard <1%, Max <2%) confirms it.
  • Worked example: $0.10 destination, FP proxy $50 → block needs p > 0.998. Sounds impossibly strict — until you note that during a burst, prevalence in the suspect segment is 50–90%, so well-calibrated scores DO clear it. Two consequences: (a) calibration is load-bearing (thresholds live in probability space — ForeCal), (b) most blocking decisions are effectively made at the episode/range level where evidence accumulates, not on lone requests.

3. Domain model — entities and where signal lives

  • Request: (app_id, to_number, channel, timestamp, client IP/ASN if the customer's SDK forwards it, destination price).
  • Destination hierarchy: number → prefix/range → carrier (MCC-MNC) → country. Reputation lives here and — key point — transfers across tenants. A prefix pumping one app will pump others. Destination-side reputation is the global, tenant-independent asset.
  • Tenant: baseline volume, geo footprint, mode setting, allowlists. Tenant-side signal is deviation from own baseline (z-scores/ratios, never absolutes — 300K tenants differ by orders of magnitude).
  • Episode: (app × prefix-or-range × time window) cluster of requests. Attacks are episodes, labels are strongest at episode level, and mitigation (block a range for an app) acts at episode level. This is the unit of analysis; requests are just its members.

4. Architecture — layered, cheap-to-expensive, inline <100ms

Why rules AND model (real reason, not vibes): attack onset is minutes; model retraining is days. Rules are the fast-twitch reflex (velocity breaker kills a 100x burst mid-flight), hot-patchable config in minutes, and explainable verbatim to a customer. The model owns the gray zone: paced attacks, new ranges, SIM farms — anything a threshold can't see. Neither substitutes for the other; latency-of-adaptation differs by 1000x.

Feature store discipline (the <100ms enabler): nothing is computed on-path except hash lookups and one GBM inference. Sliding-window velocity counters (1m/10m/1h/24h) at app, app×country, app×prefix, prefix-global, IP, ASN levels — maintained by the stream processor, incremented at request time. Hot-shard note (volunteer it): an attack IS a hot key by definition — one prefix at 2K RPS. Shard counters with random suffixing (key → key#0..15, sum on read) or local pre-aggregation with periodic flush; never let the attack melt the shard that's supposed to detect it.

Response shaping: on block, return a normal-shaped API response (don't teach the bot which requests died), don't bill the customer, log verdict + reason codes to the customer's Fraud Guard dashboard.

Fail-open: scoring timeout or Redis down → allow with rules-only floor + async flag. Never breach the latency SLO to make a fraud decision; the one exception is a confirmed-episode range which stays blocked from L0 config even if ML is dark.

5. The model layer

Model: gradient-boosted trees, per-request, CPU, sub-ms inference. Tabular features, needs frequent retrains, needs reason codes for customer disputes — GBM beats deep here on every axis that matters. (Deep learning earns nothing on 30 tabular features at 2K RPS.)

Features (grouped by what survives adversary rotation):

  • Velocity/shape: request rate vs tenant baseline at each entity level (ratios), inter-arrival regularity (bots are metronomic), burst acceleration.
  • Destination reputation: Bayesian-smoothed historical conversion rate at prefix → carrier → country with shrinkage toward parent (new prefix inherits carrier prior — cold-start for free), destination price (attackers prefer expensive routes), range age/first-seen-recency.
  • Sequentiality: entropy of number suffixes within window per app×prefix — sequential ranges are the signature of allocated test ranges.
  • Novelty: app never sent to this country/carrier before; new-range-suddenly-hot.
  • Client-side (when SDK forwards): IP/ASN reputation, IP↔destination-geo mismatch, time-of-day vs destination timezone. Make richer SDK signals an adoption incentive: Max mode works best with client context.

Live arrival streams. Top lane: human logins (Poisson — bursty, irregular). Bottom lane: bot OTP requests (fixed cadence). Inter-arrival regularity is a feature the attacker must pay to fake.

Training with regime-dependent imbalance: sample by episode (not by request) to stop one mega-attack from dominating the loss; reweight; then recalibrate post-hoc (isotonic/temperature per segment) because reweighting destroys calibration and §2 made calibration the decision substrate. Evaluate with PR-AUC and precision-at-mode-threshold per segment (geo × tenant-size), never global ROC-AUC — 99.9% AUC is trivial when 95% of traffic is obviously fine.

6. Labels — the heart of this question

Conversion is a weak per-request label but a strong episode label. Legit traffic has 20–40% natural non-conversion (abandonment, delivery failure) — so "didn't convert" ≠ fraud for one request. But 500 sends to one prefix with 0 conversions is damning. Design: label EPISODES from aggregate conversion + dispute confirmations, propagate to member requests (weak supervision), train the per-request model on that.

SIM-farm poisoning: farms complete ~30% of OTPs to launder the conversion signal. Counters: (a) conversion at 100x baseline velocity to one range is still anomalous — volume × price beats conversion rate; (b) bot conversions are timing-regular — variance of request→conversion latency is a feature; (c) where customers share post-signup signals, converted-then-dormant accounts flag fake conversions; (d) sparse dispute labels act as the audit tier that catches what conversion misses.

Selective labels / censoring — the staff-level catch, volunteer it unprompted: the moment we block, we stop observing outcomes. Three rots follow: FP rate becomes unmeasurable (blocked-legit looks identical to blocked-fraud), training data shrinks to allowed traffic (model goes stale exactly where it acts), and blocked ranges can never demonstrate rehabilitation. Fixes, all three needed:

  • ε-exploration slice: allow through a small sampled fraction of would-block traffic. This costs real money — so budget it in $ per tenant per day, and Twilio refunds exploration traffic (it's a measurement cost of the product, not the customer's fraud loss). Propensity-weight these outcomes to get unbiased fraud-rate and FP-rate estimates per mode.
  • Shadow mode for every new model/threshold before it takes the block action.
  • Dispute/appeal loop as delayed audit of blocked traffic; every upheld dispute is a confirmed FP label — the rarest, most valuable data in the system.

The exploration slice is also what makes the dashboard honest: "$ saved" extrapolated from measured fraud density with confidence intervals, defensible in an EBR — not "we blocked X messages, trust us."

7. Cold start (per-tenant — it's real, tenants onboard daily)

  • Destination reputation is global and works from day one — new tenants inherit the network's knowledge of bad ranges. This is Twilio's moat vs any single customer building in-house; say so.
  • Tenant baseline doesn't exist yet → hierarchical prior from onboarding segment (app category, declared geo footprint), tight default velocity caps, Standard mode default, Bayesian ramp of the baseline over 2–4 weeks with wider rule margins meanwhile.
  • Symmetric risk: fraudsters onboard AS tenants to probe the system — new-tenant traffic patterns feed the same episode detector.

8. Adversarial adaptation & monitoring

  • Range rotation: reputation features decay (half-life); sequentiality, velocity ratios, and IP/ASN survive rotation; the model leans on rotation-invariant features by construction (that's why §5 grouped them).
  • Pacing under the velocity radar: slow pumping still converts ≈0% — long-window conversion features and cumulative $-anomaly per destination catch what burst rules miss. The attacker can hide the burst or take the revenue, not both: pacing slashes their throughput, which is itself a win — publish that framing internally (deterrence economics: raise attacker cost-per-$).
  • Monitoring: conversion rate by segment (collapse = new attack or poisoned signal), score-distribution drift, block-rate by mode/geo, exploration-slice fraud density (recall proxy), dispute rate (precision proxy). Alert on segment-level changepoints.
  • Cadence: reputation aggregates refresh continuously (stream), model weekly + drift-triggered, rules hot-patchable in minutes.

9. Ops, product, incident path

  • Reason codes on every block (top GBM features, human-readable) → dashboard + dispute flow. Per-customer overrides: allowlists, geo permits, custom caps.
  • Incident walkthrough (legit-surge case): Brazil marketing campaign triggers Max mode. Detect: dispute spike + exploration slice shows high conversion in "blocked" segment. Remediate: auto-relax that app×country, notify customer, refund. Prevent: campaign-calendar API / pre-registration of expected surges; surges announced in advance get temporary elevated baselines.
  • Serving cost (volunteer the math): 2K RPS peak; GBM ~sub-ms CPU → a handful of pods; Redis counter cluster tens of GB; small Flink cluster. Infra well under $50K/month against fraud measured in $M/yr for single large customers — ROI is not the constraint, FP risk is. The scarce resource in this system is customer trust, not compute.

10. Thirty-second summary (close with this)

"Pumping is revenue-share fraud, so I frame every send as an expected-value decision: block when p(fraud) × message-cost exceeds (1−p) × customer's verification value — and since that value varies 100x by customer, protection modes are just presets of it. Inline: config guard → precomputed Redis features → per-tenant-normalized burst rules for onset speed → calibrated GBM for the gray zone, all under 100ms with fail-open. Labels: conversion as an episode-level weak label, disputes as the audit tier, and — because blocking censors outcomes — a refunded ε-exploration slice that keeps FP and fraud rates measurable. Destination reputation is global and transfers across all 300K tenants from day one; tenant baselines ramp hierarchically. The adversary can evade the burst rules only by pacing, which cuts their revenue — we win either way."
